# Deep Dive Passage Study

## Purpose

Use this procedure for an extended, research-quality study of a specific biblical passage when the user asks for a **deep dive**, **comprehensive exegetical study**, **research-quality passage study**, **full passage study**, or equivalent investigation.

Keep the controlling passage primary. Expand research because it materially improves interpretation, not merely because more material is available.

Do not use this procedure for ordinary narrow questions, quick word studies, or routine Passage Study unless the user requests this depth.

Do not turn the study into a sermon, devotional, course lesson, workbook, slide deck, or other downstream ministry product. Stop at study unless the user explicitly requests a downstream handoff owned by another Skill.

## 1. Operating principles

Apply these controls throughout the study:

- Let Scripture in context control the study.
- Keep the assigned passage or preaching unit explicit.
- Read the immediate literary context before widening to canonical synthesis.
- Distinguish observation, interpretation, synthesis, inference, and speculation.
- Research broadly enough to test the strongest reading, not merely confirm an initial assumption.
- Preserve meaningful alternatives when the evidence does not warrant certainty.
- Use calibrated confidence language such as `the text states`, `strongly suggests`, `likely`, `plausible`, `possible`, `interpreters differ`, or `unsupported`.
- Do not convert plausible background, verbal similarity, typology, or theological possibility into certainty.
- Do not allow a preferred theological framework to become the controlling meaning unless the passage itself supports that role.
- Keep technical material only when it materially clarifies interpretation.
- Prefer a smaller number of well-supported canonical connections over a large proof-text list.
- Do not perform the formal procedures owned by `kv-passage-faithfulness-check`, `kv-source-transparency-check`, or `kv-scripture-formatting-check` unless the user explicitly requests those audits or checks.

If project instructions or user-supplied control documents narrow the study, honor those boundaries without promoting project-specific material into global Study Engine behavior.

## 2. Assignment framing

At the beginning of the study:

1. Identify the controlling passage.
2. Delimit the literary unit that must be read with it.
3. Identify the user's specific research questions or requested emphases.
4. Identify supplied sources, project controls, and external-research expectations.
5. State the intended study depth and any material limits.
6. State a preliminary passage burden without turning it into a sermon outline.

When the user has already supplied these elements, proceed without unnecessary clarification.

## 3. Textual center and literary context

Establish what the passage is doing in its book before expanding outward.

Examine:

- literary boundaries;
- immediate context;
- book-level argument or narrative movement;
- speaker, audience, participants, and setting;
- discourse or narrative function;
- structural relationships;
- repeated words or ideas;
- contrasts and comparisons;
- cause/effect relationships;
- promises, commands, warnings, questions, or declarations;
- pronoun or address shifts;
- verbal forms and tense/aspect where material;
- quotations, allusions, images, metaphors, and intertextual signals.

Explain how these features constrain interpretation.

## 4. Observation before interpretation

Separate descriptive observation from interpretive conclusions.

For important observations, show:

- **Observation:** what is actually present in the text.
- **Interpretive significance:** why that feature matters.
- **Confidence:** how strongly the observation supports the proposed interpretation.

Do not smuggle theological conclusions into the observation stage.

## 5. Major interpretive questions

Identify only questions that materially affect the meaning of the passage.

For each major question:

1. State the question precisely.
2. Present the strongest interpretive options.
3. Give the textual, literary, grammatical, historical, or canonical evidence supporting each serious option.
4. Identify weaknesses or limiting evidence.
5. Explain what is at stake for interpretation.
6. Adopt a reading only when the evidence supports doing so.
7. State an explicit confidence level or calibrated confidence phrase.
8. Preserve unresolved alternatives when certainty is not warranted.

Do not include minor scholarly disputes that do not affect the passage burden.

## 6. Original-language analysis

Use Hebrew, Aramaic, or Greek selectively where language materially affects interpretation or the user specifically requests language work.

For significant terms or constructions, as relevant:

- give the original term and transliteration;
- identify morphology or parsing;
- examine syntax and grammatical relationships;
- describe a responsible semantic range;
- examine usage in the immediate passage and biblical book;
- compare significant Septuagint or canonical usage where relevant;
- note translation differences that materially affect interpretation;
- distinguish lexical possibility from contextual meaning;
- explain the interpretive payoff.

When Blue Letter Bible is available or explicitly requested, it may be used for interlinear, parsing, concordance, lexical navigation, and Strong's indexing. Do not treat a Strong's gloss, root meaning, or English definition as decisive evidence.

Avoid:

- root fallacies;
- illegitimate totality transfer;
- arguments based only on etymology;
- claims that `the Greek/Hebrew really means` something the syntax and context do not sustain;
- fabricated parsing, Strong's numbers, lexicon quotations, links, or source details.

## 7. Historical, cultural, literary, and geographical background

Research background only where it clarifies the passage.

For each substantive background claim, distinguish:

- established evidence;
- strong historical reconstruction;
- plausible reconstruction;
- debated reconstruction;
- speculation.

Always explain the **interpretive payoff**. Exclude colorful historical details that do not change or sharpen interpretation.

Prefer primary sources or strong scholarly sources when available. Do not present a later custom, disputed reconstruction, or secondary-source assertion as though the biblical text states it.

## 8. Old Testament and canonical connections

Trace the strongest biblical connections in order of evidentiary strength.

Investigate, where relevant:

- direct quotations;
- explicit references;
- strong allusions or echoes;
- shared vocabulary or imagery;
- narrative patterns;
- covenantal or prophetic backgrounds;
- conceptual parallels within the same author or corpus;
- later canonical texts that clarify theological significance.

Classify significant connections as:

- **Explicit** — directly quoted, cited, or clearly identified.
- **Strongly probable** — multiple textual indicators support intentional connection.
- **Plausible** — meaningful evidence exists, but intentional dependence is uncertain.
- **Thematic only** — useful canonical resonance without evidence of direct allusion.

Do not treat every shared word or concept as an intentional intertextual link.

## 9. Biblical-theological development

Place the passage within the larger biblical story only where the text and canon warrant it.

Consider relevant trajectories such as:

- creation and human vocation;
- fall and rebellion;
- covenant;
- Israel's calling;
- kingdom;
- temple and divine presence;
- exile and restoration;
- Messiah;
- nations;
- sacred space;
- spiritual conflict;
- Spirit;
- cross and resurrection;
- ascension and reign;
- church and mission;
- eschatological restoration;
- resurrection and new creation.

Distinguish:

- what the passage teaches directly;
- what its book or immediate canonical context strongly develops;
- what broader biblical theology allows as synthesis.

Consult `Theology_Framework_REVISED.md` when it is present and materially relevant. Let the framework serve the passage rather than control it.

## 10. Christological analysis

Ask how the passage responsibly relates to Jesus Christ.

Classify Christological connections where useful:

- direct messianic or Christological statement;
- New Testament-identified fulfillment;
- strong canonical trajectory culminating in Christ;
- responsible typological connection;
- broader theological synthesis;
- speculative or unsupported Christological reading.

Explain the textual and canonical basis for the connection.

Do not manufacture a Christological tie merely because one would be useful for preaching. A legitimate Christian reading may still need to say that the passage's immediate burden is not directly Christological.

## 11. Kingdom and cosmic-redemption analysis

Where the passage warrants it, ask how the text contributes to God's reign, defeat of evil, restoration of humanity, reconciliation of the nations, renewed divine presence, resurrection, or new creation.

Potential themes include:

- God's kingship;
- kingdom of God/heaven;
- Messiah's reign;
- defeat of sin and death;
- defeat or judgment of rebellious spiritual powers;
- restoration of the nations;
- renewed image-bearing vocation;
- reconciliation;
- temple/divine presence;
- resurrection;
- inheritance;
- new creation.

Use cosmic-redemption, divine-council, or spiritual-warfare categories only when the passage, its canonical context, or the user's explicit research question warrants them. Do not make unseen-realm themes controlling merely because they are theologically interesting.

## 12. Curated cross-reference map

Build a curated map rather than a reference dump.

Organize references under categories such as:

- immediate/book context;
- Old Testament background;
- lexical or conceptual parallels;
- canonical development;
- Christological fulfillment or trajectory;
- kingdom/eschatological fulfillment.

For each important reference, explain in one or two sentences **why it matters** to the controlling passage.

Prioritize interpretive relevance over quantity.

## 13. Major theological claims and support categories

For substantive conclusions, identify the kind of support behind the claim when doing so improves clarity.

Use these categories:

- **Biblical text** — directly stated or clearly entailed by the controlling passage.
- **Canonical synthesis** — supported through comparison with other biblical texts.
- **Original-language analysis** — supported by grammar, syntax, usage, or lexical evidence.
- **Historical/cultural reconstruction** — dependent on external historical evidence.
- **Theological synthesis** — doctrinal conclusion drawn from multiple texts.
- **Pastoral inference/application** — responsible implication for hearers.
- **User-provided material** — supplied notes, studies, project controls, or approved handoffs.
- **Unsupported assumption** — insufficiently grounded for confident use.

A true doctrine may still be a poor controlling lens for a passage. Keep the passage's own emphasis primary.

## 14. Interpretive guardrails

Identify readings or claims that should be rejected, qualified, or kept tentative because they:

- exceed the textual evidence;
- depend on weak lexical arguments;
- confuse background with textual meaning;
- flatten legitimate ambiguity;
- force a theological system onto the passage;
- depend on speculative typology;
- become moralistic;
- generate prosperity-style promises;
- ignore literary context;
- erase suffering, judgment, grace, or eschatological tension present in the text;
- make the passage say more than it actually says.

When a popular interpretation is weak, explain why without overstating the alternative.

## 15. Ministry implications

Move to implication only after the exegetical work is established.

Identify responsible implications for areas such as:

- faith;
- repentance;
- worship;
- hope;
- discipleship;
- Christian identity;
- community life;
- mission;
- suffering;
- obedience.

Keep implications text-shaped, grace-aware, and proportionate. Do not let application replace exegesis, and do not build a sermon outline in this section.

## 16. Final study synthesis

Conclude with a compact synthesis containing the following fields when they are materially supported:

### Passage burden

State what the passage is primarily communicating in its literary context.

### Main idea candidate

Give one clear sentence capturing the passage's central claim without sermon rhetoric.

### Key exegetical findings

List the findings that most materially affect interpretation.

### Canonical contribution

Explain what this passage contributes to the larger biblical story.

### Christological connection

State the strongest responsible connection to Christ, including limits where necessary.

### Kingdom / redemptive-historical contribution

Explain how the passage contributes to the biblical story of God's reign and restoration when warranted.

### Supported application lanes

List applications that clearly arise from the text.

### Claims requiring caution

Identify plausible ideas that should not be taught or preached as certain.

### Unresolved questions

Identify questions for which the evidence does not justify a firm conclusion.

### Material worth preserving but not preaching

Retain technical, historical, lexical, or theological findings that are valuable for research but too qualified, dense, or tangential for normal proclamation.

## 17. Source inventory and uncertainty

List significant sources actually used and state what each materially contributed.

Keep source categories distinct:

- biblical text;
- user/project sources;
- language tools and lexicons;
- historical or primary sources;
- scholarly secondary sources;
- generated synthesis.

Never invent quotations, page numbers, bibliographic details, source attributions, URLs, lexical data, historical claims, or citations.

If exact source details cannot be verified, paraphrase cautiously or mark the source detail as unresolved.

If the study depends on external research, identify the major external sources actually used. Do not pad the source inventory with sources that did not materially affect the study.

## 18. Output structure

Use this as the default deep-dive output shape, adapting sections when a category is not materially relevant:

1. Study thesis / preliminary passage burden
2. Passage boundaries and literary context
3. Textual observations
4. Major interpretive questions and options
5. Original-language findings
6. Historical/cultural/literary background
7. Old Testament and canonical connections
8. Biblical-theological development
9. Christological analysis
10. Kingdom / cosmic-redemption analysis
11. Curated cross-reference map
12. Major theological claims
13. Interpretive guardrails
14. Ministry implications
15. Final study synthesis
16. Source inventory
17. Unresolved questions

Do not create empty sections for categories that do not materially apply. State briefly when a requested framework is not well supported by the passage.

## 19. Stopping rule

Finish at the study layer.

If the user asks to convert the completed study into a Sermon Builder handoff, invoke `kv-study-to-sermon-handoff`.

If the user asks to convert it into a Course Builder handoff, invoke `kv-study-to-course-handoff`.

If the user requests formal faithfulness, source-transparency, or Scripture-formatting review, route to the dedicated Skill.

If the user asks only for the deep-dive study, do not append a sermon outline, devotional, lesson plan, slides, media plan, or publishing package.
